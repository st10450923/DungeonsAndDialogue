# Critical Engagement with Feedback, Dungeons and Dialogue

**Event:** Makers Massive  
**Submitted by:** Ember Willow Jones, St10450923  
**Word Count:** 400

### What Did I Expect?

Going into Makers Massive, I anticipated feedback focused on the LLM integration itself, latency, hallucination issues, pipeline consistency. I did not expect it to centre as heavily on presentation and UX. The feedback was also far more positive than I expected, with people genuinely enjoying interacting with the NPCs in a way I personally didn't. Knowing the prompts and systems behind the game prevented me from enjoying the puzzle-solving aspect, but the showcase encouraged me to keep working on it despite initially disliking the project. I've come to see that the unpredictability of LLM responses is actually a positive feature, functioning like the dice of a board game, despite my personal views on generative AI being overwhelmingly negative.



### What Surprised Me?

The most surprising outcome was how much weight attendees placed on surface-level polish. Art, sound, and UI responsiveness drew more commentary than the LLM behaviour itself, suggesting that for a general creative-tech audience, the experiential layer matters as much as the technical architecture underneath it. The suggestion of voice input was also unexpected — it indicated attendees were engaging imaginatively with the concept rather than just evaluating what was in front of them. Equally surprising was the feedback about room density.



### What Feedback Did I Choose Not to Implement, and Why?

Voice input was not manageable within the remaining timeline. Integrating a reliable speech-to-text pipeline would require significant additional engineering, testing, and UI redesign — attempting it under pressure risks destabilising existing systems without delivering a working feature. A full art rework was similarly out of scope; the game is a prototype demonstrating LLM integration, not a commercially polished product.



### Evaluation of Feasibility

Keyboard shortcuts, a restart-to-menu button, and a functional quit button were prioritised as small, low-risk UX improvements. NPC personality randomisation required moderate prompt engineering effort but posed no risk to existing functionality. The Piper TTS voiceover was ultimately removed due to time constraints, though attempts remain in the GitHub history.



### Final Judgement

The feedback was practically useful and grounded in actual player experience. Without adequate UX support, the mechanical systems fell somewhat flat and made the game frustrating to play — something I had underweighted. The decision to decline voice input and a full art rework was a reasoned feasibility call, not dismissiveness. People enjoyed the game for what it was and wanted an improved version of it, so I chose to additively strengthen what existed rather than rework or expand systems entirely.The addition of new room content and art assets reflects this philosophy directly — the core loop remains unchanged, but the world around it now better supports the experience players responded to at the showcase.
